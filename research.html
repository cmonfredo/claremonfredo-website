research & writing

Rethinking performance practice

How can performers be creative in the realization of written music?

Motivated by my training and career as a cellist, I examine interpretation and individuality in classical music — how performers negotiate the constraints and freedoms of this tradition. My research approaches this question from several directions — performance analysis, music cognition, and practice-led research — using Bach’s Cello Suites as a shared case study.

Research project

The Sounds of Tradition and Individuality in Classical Music

A novel quantitative approach

My research investigates how classical musicians express individuality in performance — and where, perhaps unknowingly, they don’t. Through quantitative and qualitative analysis of 23 contemporary recordings of Bach’s Fourth Cello Suite Prelude, my doctoral work showed that while cellists sound strikingly different from one another in immediately audible musical features like tempo and bow stroke, their musical shaping — the phrasing and dynamics widely considered the heart of interpretation — is remarkably uniform.

In revealing the often invisible constraints of performance tradition, this work calls for greater attention to underexplored dimensions of musical identity, including timbre and bow use, and for a broader conception of interpretive freedoms that looks beyond current performance practice constraints.

Imitation as a tool for innovation

Finding new ways to perform Bach’s Fourth Cello Suite

Since the nineteenth century, classical performance training has prioritized fidelity to the score and discouraged copying recordings in the process of learning notated music. Yet recent performance scholarship suggests that current practice allows far less novelty than even the score permits, and that studying recordings can unlock performer agency.

This lecture-recital demonstrates a three-part, practice-led method of deliberate imitation: developing technical mastery of the notes as written; systematically internalizing the idiosyncratic details of a specific recording, such as its ornaments, bowings, and rhythms; and expanding those gestures through practice into a new, personal interpretation. By copying recordings that fall outside the standard modern cello discography, Clare distances herself from ingrained habits — and shows that imitation need not tether performers to another person’s ideas, but can help them reread canonical scores in new ways.

Case study 1 · Allemande

Paolo Pandolfo’s viola da gamba transcription

Pandolfo’s gamba transcription of the Cello Suites takes a highly flexible, chordal approach to Bach. Mimicking his unusual harmonizations on the cello prompts a re-evaluation of implied harmonies, timing, and bow distribution.

Case study 2 · Courante

Gavriel Lipkind’s rewritten repeats

Lipkind subverts traditional approaches to Bach by boldly rewriting the music on repeats, introducing new notes, motives, and even atypical rhythms. Clare’s version adopts several of his embellishments and adds virtuosic elements of her own.

Case study 3 · Bourrée II

Jordi Savall’s variation

On his album Les Voix Humaines, Savall plays the Bourrée in its entirety and then performs a complete variation on its theme. Clare transcribes Savall’s variation for the cello and composes an additional variation of her own.
